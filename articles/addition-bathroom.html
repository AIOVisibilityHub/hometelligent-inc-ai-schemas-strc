Preparing to build a new bathroom requires verifying physical and municipal practicalities before requesting formal estimates. Use this checklist to get your property and expectations in order before your contractor walkthrough.

  • Property survey or plot plan — Confirms exterior boundary lines and easements if your new bathroom expands your existing exterior footprint.
  • Location of the main sewer line — Identifies where existing waste stacks run so you can evaluate the slope needed for new toilet and shower drains.
  • Main electrical panel capacity rating — Determines whether your service panel has open breaker slots for dedicated GFCI circuits, lighting, and ventilation.
  • Water heater capacity and age record — Confirms whether your current water heater can supply additional demand without running cold during peak family routines.
  • Exterior wall venting route — Pinpoints an unobstructed exterior path through a wall or roof for a dedicated bathroom exhaust fan venting outside.
  • Measure available interior clear dimensions — Verifies that planned fixture layouts meet required clearances in front of toilets, sinks, and shower doors.
  • Municipal permit and zoning records — Identifies whether prior unpermitted additions exist that could complicate building department plan reviews.
  • List of must-have fixtures and accessibility needs — Clarifies whether you require curbless walk-in showers, double vanities, or specific plumbing allowances.

How to use this checklist

Work through these items prior to your initial contractor consultation. Having this information organized helps your design-build team quickly assess feasibility, plan drain runs, and provide an accurate initial proposal.

Homeowners with growing households frequently ask whether they need to hire a full general contracting firm or simply bring in an independent plumber for a new bathroom.

When Professional Guidance Helps

If you are simply replacing an existing vanity and toilet in the exact same footprint, a specialized trade contractor may be sufficient. However, adding an entirely new bathroom—whether by carving out interior closet space or building an exterior bump-out—demands structural framing, electrical routing, exterior ventilation, and complex plumbing ties.

You should hire a licensed general contractor whenever

  • New drain lines, vent stacks, or water supply trunks must tie into the main house systems.
  • Walls are moving, structural headers are being cut, or floor joists must be notched for plumbing.
  • Your project requires multi-trade building permits, plan checks, and scheduled city inspections.
  • Waterproofing systems are needed for curbless showers or custom tile pans to prevent subfloor rot.
  • The location sits far from existing plumbing stacks, requiring precise subfloor fall calculations.

Handling concealed conditions and structural framing requires single-source oversight to ensure trades do not conflict inside small spaces.

Your contractor checks subfloor joist orientation to ensure waste pipes can maintain adequate drainage slope without compromising structural framing.

Draft detailed architectural and plumbing schematics showing pipe sizes, venting pathways, and electrical circuit loads. Submit these documents to your municipal building department to obtain necessary building, plumbing, and electrical permits before touching any walls.

Open existing walls and floors, remove unnecessary partitions, and frame new walls, pocket door frames, and shower enclosures. Any necessary joist sistering, subfloor reinforcement, or structural header installations take place during this phase to support new plumbing and tile loads.

How to Know if Your Home Has the Capacity for an Extra Bathroom

How do you know if your home has the plumbing and electrical capacity for an extra bathroom?

Determining whether your home can support an additional bathroom comes down to three factors: drain elevation, water supply volume, and electrical panel capacity. You cannot assume an extra bath can simply tie into the nearest pipe.

First, check your drain geometry. Waste lines rely on gravity, requiring a minimum downward slope to function without clogging. If your new bathroom is situated far from the main sewer cleanout, getting the proper pipe pitch beneath your floor framing or through a concrete slab requires strategic routing. In crawlspaces, joist direction and depth dictate where large drain pipes can safely pass without weakening structural framing.

Second, evaluate your water supply and venting. Multiple fixtures running simultaneously require adequate water pressure and volume. An older half-inch main supply line may need upgrading to three-quarter inch to maintain steady shower pressure when other taps run. Additionally, every new trap must connect to an approved plumbing vent stack terminating through the roof.

Finally, review your electrical panel. Modern bathrooms require dedicated 20-amp circuits to safely power hair dryers, lighting, and ventilation without tripping breakers. If your breaker box is fully loaded, you may need a subpanel or a main panel upgrade.

When Professional Guidance Helps

Evaluating pipe slopes, structural joists, and electrical demands requires an experienced eye. A licensed general contractor ensures your plumbing and power infrastructure meets code before you commit to fixture selections.
