ADU Bathroom Plumbing Mistakes to Avoid During Rough-In Construction

Installing a fully functional bathroom in a detached or attached ADU brings unique plumbing demands. Because spaces are compact and must tie into main sewer runs, small oversights can create severe consequences down the road.

Watch out for these critical plumbing mistakes during bathroom rough-in

  • Inadequate drain slope. Waste pipes require a consistent slope to drain by gravity. Setting drains with shallow falls leads to chronic clogs that are difficult to fix once slabs are poured or floors are finished.
  • Poor shower waterproofing details. Relying solely on cement board without an approved waterproofing membrane behind tile invites mold, wood rot, and subfloor damage over time.
  • Undersized water supply lines. ADUs sharing an existing service line may suffer severe pressure drops when multiple fixtures run. Proper line sizing and pressure checks prevent cold shower surprises.
  • Unvented exhaust fans. Discharging bathroom exhaust fans into crawlspaces or attics instead of routing ductwork directly outside causes moisture accumulation and framing decay.
  • Covering work before city inspections. Closing walls with drywall or pouring concrete over rough plumbing before an inspector signs off usually results in mandatory wall tear-outs.

Connecting an ADU bathroom requires careful calculations for sewer capacity, trenching slopes, and local code compliance. Working with a licensed design-build general contractor protects your investment and ensures every fixture operates smoothly.

Adding a bathroom to an ADU requires balancing tight floor space, accessibility needs, and technical code requirements. Use this checklist to review your layout and utilities before construction begins.

  • Sewer tie-in elevation confirmed — Verifying the main line depth ensures your ADU waste pipes maintain necessary gravity fall without requiring an ejector pump.
  • Main water line capacity verified — Checking water volume and pressure confirms existing supply lines can support additional bathroom fixtures.
  • Exterior wall exhaust duct route mapped — Ducting bath exhaust directly outside prevents condensation buildup inside wall cavities or ceilings.
  • Electrical GFCI circuit planned — Outlets near wet zones require dedicated ground-fault circuit interrupter protection according to electrical safety standards.
  • Backing installed for grab bars and vanities — Solid wood blocking behind wall framing ensures heavy mirrors, floating sinks, and safety bars mount securely.
  • Shower waterproofing assembly specified — Selecting an approved moisture barrier prevents water from penetrating wall framing behind tiled shower enclosures.
  • Dedicated shutoff valves planned — Placing shutoffs for every sink, toilet, and shower valve makes maintenance possible without shutting off water to the entire ADU.
  • Minimum fixture clearances measured — Ensuring code-compliant space between toilets, vanity counters, and shower doors guarantees comfortable everyday movement.

How to Use This Checklist

Review each of these items during the architectural planning stage and again before framing begins. Addressing utility connections and space clearances early avoids costly change orders and delays once rough plumbing starts.

Installing a bathroom in an accessory dwelling unit is more involved than a simple fixture swap. Because it involves creating brand-new utility connections and meeting strict building standards, certain conditions call for experienced general contracting help.

Signals you need a professional for your ADU bathroom

  • New sewer trenching across your yard. Connecting the ADU to your home's main lateral requires precise trench slope calculations, utility locates, and secure tie-ins.
  • Concrete slab penetrations. If your ADU sits on a concrete slab, cutting channels for drainpipes and re-sealing concrete requires specialized structural awareness.
  • Limited sewer elevation. If the ADU sits lower than the city sewer connection, engineered pumping systems or sewage ejectors become mandatory.
  • Curbless or zero-threshold shower designs. Building modern, accessible curbless showers requires structural subfloor recessed framing and flawless pan waterproofing to avoid leaks.
  • Unpermitted space conversions. Bringing an existing detached garage or bonus room up to legal dwelling standards requires evaluating wall structures, ventilation, and plumbing access.

What to Expect During the Process

A licensed builder manages the plan submittals, coordinates with municipal inspectors, oversees rough-in trades, and applies waterproofing assemblies before installing tile, vanities, and fixtures.
