Thinking about an attached ADU? It's a fantastic way to add space and value, but not every home is an ideal fit. An attached ADU means it's structurally connected to your main house or another accessory building, and that connection brings specific considerations.

You'll need to figure out if your property has enough space, if the existing structure can support the changes, and how things like utility connections and separate entrances will work out. It's not just about adding square footage; it's about integrating it seamlessly and legally with your current home.

You'll also want to consider privacy for both units and how the new addition will affect the overall look and feel of your property.

What Can Change the Right Choice

Whether an attached ADU is the right move for you often hinges on a few big factors. For starters, the specific zoning and building codes in your city are huge — they dictate everything from maximum size to setback requirements. Don't forget utility capacity; your existing electrical panel or sewer line might need upgrades to handle another dwelling unit.

If your project involves knocking down load-bearing walls or significantly changing the foundation, you're looking at a more complex build that needs expert structural engineering. And privacy is key; if a separate entrance or soundproofing is a challenge with your current layout, an attached ADU might require more creative solutions. Finally, think about future flexibility.

Are you planning for aging parents, adult children, or rental income? The intended use can really shape the design and what permits you'll need.

Getting your attached ADU built is exciting, but the permit process can feel like a maze. It’s not just one permit you need; it could be a whole bunch, depending on your local jurisdiction and the specifics of your project. Beyond the main building permit, you might need separate permits for electrical, plumbing, mechanical work, and even sewer connections.

Each Bay Area city has its own rhythm, and rules can change. You can’t just start construction whenever you feel ready. Starting work before your permits are officially issued is a major no-no and can lead to costly delays or fines.

We’ve seen folks get tripped up by underestimating the need for specialized trade permits or thinking an attached ADU is just a simple remodel. It’s often more involved, especially if structural changes or fire-separation details are part of the plan.

What to Expect During the Process

Expect a multi-stage journey when navigating permits for your attached ADU. First, you'll want to verify your site's eligibility and local zoning rules. Next, you'll need detailed plans – architectural, structural, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing – often prepared by qualified designers or engineers.

Once those are ready, you'll submit permit applications and pay fees to your local building department. This review process can take time, depending on how complex your plans are and how busy the department is. During construction, inspectors will visit your site at various stages to ensure everything is up to code.

Finally, you’ll need to obtain final approval or occupancy documentation before anyone can actually move into or use the ADU. Remember, the timing for all this can vary a lot, so don't schedule your move-in date until those final approvals are in hand.

  • When multiple unrelated trades are involved: If your project touches on framing, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC all at once, a licensed general contractor can coordinate everyone efficiently.

Checklist: What to Review Before Building an Attached ADU Addition

Connecting an accessory dwelling unit directly to your existing house requires careful investigation of your current structure and shared utility lines. Use this checklist to review critical structural, mechanical, and site items before finalizing construction plans.

  • Existing foundation condition — An engineer must inspect the main foundation to confirm it can handle the tied-in loads without shifting or cracking.
  • Main electrical panel capacity — Verifying whether your current service has enough amperage avoids unexpected power outages once the new suite is running.
  • Sewer lateral depth and flow line — Confirming your sewer pipe slope ensures wastewater from the new addition drains properly into the municipal main.
  • Shared wall fire separation — Building codes require specific one-hour fire-rated drywall assemblies between the primary home and the attached unit to protect occupants.
  • Independent exterior entrance access — Local rules mandate a separate, unobstructed outside door so the ADU operates as an independent living unit.
  • Bedroom egress window dimensions — Checking sill heights and net clear opening sizes ensures sleeping rooms meet emergency exit safety codes.
  • Acoustic insulation details — Installing dense mineral wool batting or resilient channel systems between shared walls prevents sound transfer between households.
  • HVAC and ventilation isolation — Dedicated heating, cooling, and bathroom exhaust systems keep moisture and indoor air quality manageable in both living areas.
