First things first, figure out which city or county agency handles permits for your property (like Alameda County or the City of Berkeley). Research their specific ADU ordinances and permit submittal requirements. Some areas have separate planning and building reviews, while others combine them, which changes your initial approach.

Before you submit anything, gather all necessary documents: architectural and structural plans, Title 24 energy calculations, application forms, and any water/sewer or address documentation. Double-check that everything is complete and consistent – this is crucial, as incomplete submissions are a major cause of delays.

Once your package is ready, submit it according to your jurisdiction's guidelines. After submission, the agency will perform a completeness screening. California state law says they generally have 60 days to approve or deny a complete ADU application when an existing dwelling is on the lot, so a complete application is key.

Expect to receive comments and requests for corrections from plan reviewers. Transmit these comments promptly to your design team (architect, engineer) and work together to prepare revised drawings and documents. Addressing corrections thoroughly the first time minimizes review cycles.

Some projects, especially those with special conditions like coastal zones or unique utility needs, might require additional approvals from other agencies. Once all reviews are done and approved, you'll need to pay the required permit fees to finalize the process. This can include impact fees or utility connection fees.

With all approvals in hand and fees paid, your permit will be issued! You can then begin construction. An in-law suite can range from a simple bedroom and bathroom addition to a full-blown Accessory Dwelling Unit (ADU) with its own kitchen, laundry, and separate entrance. Accessibility is another huge factor, especially if the suite is for an older adult or someone with mobility challenges. Also, consider the privacy needs of both the occupants of the suite and the main house. Sound control, separate entrances, and thoughtful layout design can help create comfortable boundaries.

What Can Change the Right Choice

The intended occupant's needs are paramount. For an aging parent, accessibility and proximity to care might be key, whereas for an adult child, independence and privacy could be higher priorities. Your existing home’s structure and lot size also play a big role.

Do you have space for an addition, or is an internal conversion (like a basement or garage) more feasible? Local zoning and building codes are critical, as they dictate what’s possible in terms of size, independent utilities, and even parking requirements for ADUs. Many homeowners assume it's just a cosmetic remodel, but any change that makes the space habitable typically requires specific permits. You can't just throw up some drywall and call it a day!

Most jurisdictions view converting a garage to a living space as a change of use, necessitating a building permit. This isn't just bureaucracy; it's about ensuring your new space is safe, up to code, and doesn't create future problems for you or your property. Beyond the general building permit, you'll likely need separate permits for electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work if you're adding outlets, moving fixtures, or installing heating/cooling.

These permits ensure that specialized trades complete their work safely and according to current regulations. During construction, various inspections will occur: framing, rough electrical, rough plumbing, rough mechanical, insulation, and finally, a final inspection. Each inspection ensures that the work meets code before the next phase can proceed.
